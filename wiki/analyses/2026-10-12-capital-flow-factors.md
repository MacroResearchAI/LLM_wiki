# Factors affecting capital flows

## Question

What factors affect capital flow?

## Answer

The wiki only identifies factors for the 1990–91 surge of capital inflows to Latin America. It does not state a general account of what moves capital flows in other places or periods.

For that episode, [Latin American capital inflows](../topics/latin-american-capital-inflows.md) treats the surge as largely an external shock. [Calvo, Leiderman, and Reinhart, capital inflows (1993)](../sources/1993-03-01-calvo-capital-inflows.md) names four external factors: lower US interest rates, the US recession, swings in the US balance of payments (1990 and 1991 were the first net private capital outflows from the United States after eight years of net inflows), and 1990 US market-access rules (Regulation S and Rule 144A).

Domestic reforms, including external-debt restructuring, were necessary. Without them, the scale of the inflows would have been hard to attract. They do not explain why inflows moved together across countries. Terms-of-trade declines of 1.2 percent in 1990 and 5.2 percent in 1991 are too small to explain the surge. Foreign factors account for about half of monthly real-exchange-rate forecast-error variance in most of ten countries over January 1988–November 1991, and they matter more where domestic policy was stable.

[Sterilized intervention](../concepts/sterilized-intervention.md) can keep an inflow going by holding up the domestic-foreign interest differential. [Real exchange rate](../concepts/real-exchange-rate.md) appreciation is an accompanying result of the inflow, not a cause named by the wiki.

## Gaps

- No evidence on capital flows outside the 1990–91 Latin American episode.
- [Neutral rate](../concepts/neutral-rate.md) and [US monetary policy stance](../topics/us-monetary-policy-stance.md) do not connect those subjects to capital flows.
- The wiki does not say how much of the 1990–91 inflow was short-term "hot money," or how large an outflow would follow if US rates rose.
